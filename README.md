## Redline Rush

A roguelike traffic-weaving racer built in **Godot 4** (GDScript). You drive an endless four-lane highway: close passes and late lane changes at speed score the most, and each stage ends in a boss race.

### Open and run

1. Install **Godot 4.3 or newer** (the standard build, not .NET) from https://godotengine.org/download.
2. In the Project Manager choose **Import**, pick this folder's `project.godot`, then press **Run** (F5).

There are no asset files to download: the cars, road, trees, UI and sounds are all generated in code.

Every player and boss car has its own body design: a hatchback, a sport sedan, a notchback and a fastback muscle car, a compact performance saloon, a mid-engined supercar, a rear-engined sports coupe and a V12 hypercar. The paint has a clear coat that reflects the sky and horizon, metallic flake that catches the sun up close, and tinted glass that turns into a mirror at shallow angles. Wheels have big alloys with concave spokes, low-profile tyres, brake discs and calipers. Lamps have projector lenses and LED running lights. Cars further than 40 m away switch to a lighter model, so the highway stays smooth on phones.

Each car has its own engine sound, generated live from a small engine model: cylinder count (from the hatch's four to the Last Exit's twelve), RPM through six gears with real shift points, throttle load, pops when you lift off and turbo whine and blow-off on the turbo cars. On and off throttle use different combustion sounds, every firing varies slightly, the exhaust pipe resonates, and you hear crank rumble at low revs, gearbox whine and an ignition-cut bark on upshifts. The HUD shows your gear and an RPM bar under the speedometer.

### Controls

| Action | Phone | Desktop |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Accelerate | Hold the accelerator pedal (bottom right) | Up / W |
| Brake | Hold the brake pedal (bottom left) | Down / S / Space |
| Steer | Swipe left or right anywhere else on the screen (how far your finger moves from where it landed is how far you steer) | Hold Left / Right |
| Pause | Pause button, or Android Back | Esc / P |

The car drives freely, like a real one: let go of the steering and the wheels self-centre, but the car keeps going the way it points, so you straighten up yourself. Nothing pulls it back into a lane. **Steering assist** (Settings › Controls, off by default) gently straightens the car up to the road when you let go, without snapping it to a lane.

### Cameras

Tap **CAM** (top right) to switch between **Chase**, **Hood** and **Cockpit** views. The cockpit is a full interior:
- **Instruments:** a real instrument panel: a rev counter and a speedometer with numbered, backlit faces (km/h with a small mph ring, and fuel and temperature bars; the rev counter's scale and red band follow each car's engine), a bezel on each, tapered needles that stand off the face and throw a shadow on it, a faint reflection on the glass, a window between the dials with the gear and a large speed readout, a row of warning lamps and, on the track cars, shift lights; and a lit sat-nav and radio screen with its own night-mode map. Each car has its own dial design in its own colours: modern, sport, classic cream with serif numerals, bold retro white, plain, or segmented track style.
- **Controls:** a steering wheel that turns: an oval-section leather rim, fuller where the hands go, with a seam of contrast stitching round it, tapered spokes with lit buttons (or slotted metal spokes on the classic wheels), and a rounded airbag hub with a badge, held by gloved hands whose fingers wrap round the rim; and a gear lever that moves through the gears.
- **Cabin:** round turbine, slim strip or bladed air vents (by car), a demister slot along the windscreen, knurled climate knobs with a row of buttons, trim strips in carbon fibre, open-pore wood or brushed metal, seats with headrests, a rear bench, door panels, sun visors and a mirror. Plastics and leather take a soft sheen from the sky, and the roof and pillars darken where they meet the glass, like a seal in shadow.

### Settings

Open **Settings** from the main menu or the pause menu. It is a sheet with a heading and four tabs at the top, a part that scrolls in the middle and a **Done** button that stays at the bottom whatever the page (it used to be at the end of a long scroll), each page split into labelled groups with a short explanation under every option: **Controls** (steering method, sensitivity, steering response, steering assist, tilt direction, dead zones, pedal size, vibration, on-screen layout), **Display** (orientation, graphics, frame rate, speed units, mirror, and **Audio**: sound on or off, a **Music** slider for the boss and police chase songs and a **Gameplay** slider for the engine, tyres, wind, crashes, scoring and menu ticks, each 0 to 100% and saved; the two are separate audio buses, `Music` and `SFX`, set by `EngineAudio.set_volumes`) and **Dev** (developer tools). **Camera ›** is not a page: it opens the camera adjuster below. On a wide screen the title and the tabs share a line, so more of the page shows.

From the main menu the settings open over the garage (they used to take you out to the highway, with your car's engine running); only the camera adjuster shows the road, to see the camera on it, and it comes back to the garage. **Pausing a run silences everything** (engine, tyres, wind, traffic, music) until you resume.

The main menu is your **garage**: a dark showroom workshop with your car standing on a glass-topped turntable, its reflection in the floor, walls of backlit slats, long light bars in the ceiling and an amber LED ring round the turntable. The paint and glass reflect that room (while the garage shows, the car shaders switch to a studio environment through the global shader parameter `env_studio`), and the camera frames the car in whatever part of the screen the menu panel leaves free (above the panel when the phone is upright, to its left when it is on its side). It eases between views when you change tab (a front three-quarter shot for **Cars**, higher for **Paint**, close on the front wheel for **Wheels**, from behind for **Upgrades**), circles slowly round a car nobody is touching, swoops in when the garage opens, and a different car slides in with a flash of light, from the side the list is going: the next car comes in from the right of the screen and the old one leaves to the left, the previous car the other way round (along the screen's own sideways direction, whichever way the camera is turned round the car). Drag to look around it. The main menu's panel shows the car's name, fuel and stats (with your upgrades) and leads with **Start run**, with **Cars**, **Workshop** and **Settings** under it and **How to play** on its own screen. **Cars** shows one car at a time, with arrows (or a swipe sideways across the card: a swipe to the left brings the next car) to go through all twelve (the locked boss cars too, to look at; a car that is not on sale yet stands under a dark cover), a row of twelve dots for where you are in the list and which cars you have, the car's numbers with how each compares with the car you drive, and one button for what you can do with it: select, buy (with what you are short of, if you are) or locked (with the boss to beat). The **Workshop** works on your current car: **Upgrades** (fitted to that car only: each car is upgraded separately, and older saves keep the upgrades they had on every car they own), **Paint** (39 colours plus factory, free, and seven finishes: gloss, semi-gloss, matte, metallic, pearlescent, candy and chrome; see Paint finishes) and **Wheels** (fourteen designs and sixteen finishes, or factory; see Wheels). Paint and wheels are saved per car and used in your runs. The garage has its own camera, lights and render layer, so the highway never shows through, and the road simulation pauses while you're in it. It is lighter to draw than the workshop it replaces: the room is a few dozen merged meshes and the floor reflection uses the light version of the car, so a frame is about 330 draw calls and 160,000 triangles (`--perf`, on software Vulkan) against 780 and 400,000 before, and the sun's shadow maps, which drew the car twice for nothing, and the 3D view behind the world map are switched off. The pause menu leads with **Resume** and keeps **End run** apart at the bottom, and the run-over screen leads with **Run again**.
- **Camera adjuster:** **Settings › Camera** puts the settings sheet away and two slim bars over the game, so the whole view can be seen while it is adjusted (the sheet covered nearly all of it). The **top bar** has the three views (**Chase**, **Hood**, **Cockpit**) and the field of view and angle; the **bottom bar** the height and distance (forward and back for hood and cockpit) and **Reset** (this view's defaults), **Shake** (camera shake on or off) and **Done**. Each slider's name and value share a line above it, so a bar is about an eighth of the screen's height: in portrait the bars take a quarter of it between them and the game has the rest, in landscape each bar is one row. The sliders move the real camera as you drag them (the game keeps drawing, the car on the road, from the main menu or the pause menu), and the play HUD is put away while it is open so that nothing but the bars is over the view. **Tap the view to put the bars away** and see the whole picture (a line under the top bar says so for the first few seconds); tap again to bring them back. Done, the back button and the edge swipe return to Settings. `--open=settings:camera` opens it on start.
- **Imported cars:** the Kestrel, Vanta GT, Bruiser, Copperhead, Halcyon, Blue Static, Night Marshal and Last Exit, and the traffic SUV, van and lorry, are now your own models (`tools/models/` sources, `assets/models/car_*.mesh`, each with a lighter distance version). Each keeps its cabin style, and the cockpit is fitted from the model's measured roof, windows and wheels and then clamped inside its real shell, so nothing pokes through. The traffic lorry is a three-axle tractor and trailer (its box takes the traffic colour, the cab a second colour, plus the fleet name), and traffic models get a lighter high-detail mesh and a decimated distance version so a busy road stays fast. The generated hatch and saloon remain for traffic until their models arrive.
- **Car models:** every player, boss and traffic car (apart from the imported Comet and Hornet, and the van and lorry) is forged as a detailed mesh by `tools/carforge.py`: smooth curved bodywork with rounded bumpers, real glass with black window surrounds and frit, LED head and tail lamps with projectors and light bars, mesh, slatted or kidney grilles, lower intakes, bonnet louvres, side intakes, diffusers, shut lines, flush handles, mirrors, wipers, shark-fin aerial, exhaust tips, and each car's spoiler or wing. Boot lips and ducktails are swept into the bodywork itself, every detail follows the rounded nose and tail, lamps wrap only the corners, and grilles and intakes are sized to the nose they sit on. Nearby cars use the full-detail version and switch to a light one beyond 22 m; your own car is always full detail. The glass is tinted, and mirrors the sky at a slant, but it is clearly see-through head-on: 58% of what is behind a window shows through (it was 26%, which read as solid dark panes), so the seats, the dashboard and the driver's hands are easy to see from outside (`clarity` in `shaders/car_glass.gdshader`).
- **Cockpit view, like looking out of a real car:** the roof, A-pillars and door frames are the car's own bodywork seen from inside (so they sit exactly where the model has them, with clean edges round the windscreen and side windows, a headliner colour per car, and dimmed at night), with the rear-view mirror turned towards you. The seat goes where the roof is highest and your head just clears it; the camera looks a touch below the road so the wheel and dials are in view. The dash is laid out from the driver's eye: its top ends at the windscreen base, the instrument pod and dials sit under the sight line over it, and the wheel sits under the dials, so the road and bonnet are never hidden. There are no driver's hands or arms in the view: just the wheel, the dash and the road. Low cars (Halcyon, Blue Static, Last Exit) seat you a little towards the middle, under the highest part of the roof.
- **Interiors:** from outside, each car shows its own modelled interior through the glass (see The cars); the cockpit camera swaps in the game's driver's view of it, set in `CABINS` in `scripts/car_model.gd`, with the working instruments, seen from where the car's own seat puts the driver's eyes. Colours, seats (comfort seats or one-piece buckets with harness slots), steering wheel (round, flat-bottomed or a yoke, with two to four spokes and an optional twelve-o'clock mark), screen (a wide screen, a tall floating tablet, or a small radio with knobs), gauge colours, dash trim (aluminium, wood, carbon or body-bright) and extras all differ: blue cloth and a tablet in the Pulse, red-stitched black in the Vector, yellow buckets in the Hornet, tan leather with a two-spoke wheel in the Comet, plain grey cloth and a radio in the Kestrel, beige and wood in the Vanta, black vinyl with a thin chrome-spoked wheel and three dash-top gauges in the Bruiser, white buckets with cyan lighting in the Halcyon, saddle leather and gauge pods in the Copperhead, blue-stitched black in the Night Marshal, electric-blue buckets in the Blue Static, and red leather, a yoke and a roll cage in the Last Exit. Every cockpit is measured against its body's real roof, sides and tail and kept inside them. The detail is made two ways: the dial faces, the screens' maps and the trim finishes are artwork baked by `tools/make_cockpit_art.py` into `assets/textures/cockpit/` (the colours come from `CABINS` and the rev counter's scale from each car's engine redline, so they cannot drift from the game), and everything with a shape (needles, bezels, the wheel, the hands, vents, knobs, trim strips) is built in `scripts/cabin_parts.gd`. Measured with `--perf`, the player's car went from 56 objects and 135,700 triangles to 36 objects and 140,600 triangles, so the cabin is no dearer to draw than before. A yoke sits lower than a round wheel, so its top bar does not hide the speed readout.
- **Starter cars:** every new garage starts with the **Pulse Hatch**, a hot hatch with a roof spoiler, and the first car to buy is the **Vector GS** ($600), a turbocharged sports saloon with a boot spoiler. Both are imported 3D models with see-through glass, the full cabin and cockpit view, their own number plates, brake lights and indicators. Their texture's shut lines, handles and fuel flap are carved into the bodywork as real trim, the black band low on the sides is body colour above a gloss-black sill, and the cockpit is measured against the real shell (roof, glass and sides) so nothing pokes through. After them come the **Hornet Compact** ($800) and the **Comet Coupe** ($1,000), also imported models, then the Kestrel ($1,200). Older saves get the Pulse added and keep the cars they had.
- **Cockpit frames (windscreen, side windows and pillars):** in the cockpit view the windscreen and side windows were soft, lumpy blobs and the pillars between them shapeless slivers, worst on the Comet and Halcyon. The car meshes are split into paint and glass triangle by triangle, so the edge of a window is ragged (teeth and tabs of a few centimetres), and the old openings were a picture of that glass blurred three times (the blur melted the teeth but left a wavering outline, and the edge itself was soft). Now `tools/make_inner.py` takes the glass patch of each opening, cleans it, takes its **convex hull** (so a windscreen is a windscreen: straight sides, no tabs, no notches) and rounds the corners (about 4 cm), shrinks it 2 cm so there is frame all round, and stores it as a **signed distance field** (1 cm texels, 128 is the edge, 6 cm either side) instead of a picture of the opening. The shell shader (`CarModel._inner_shader`) cuts along the 0 line, which stays a clean curve at any size, and draws a 3 cm dark rubber seal along it, so every opening has a crisp, defined edge. Where the windscreen and the side window come within 5.5 cm of each other (the wrap-round cars: Pulse, Vector, Kestrel, Vanta, Copperhead, Marshal) neither is cut, so there is always an A-pillar at least that wide (without it the openings met in a point). The shell also carries smooth vertex normals now (3 bytes a vertex), so the roof, pillars and seam between headliner and trim shade smoothly and the roof darkens towards its rails, instead of facets of flat colour. The opening is about the size of the glass (the windscreen's 0.45 m2 on the Halcyon, 0.73 m2 on the Pulse); the files are no bigger. Checked by rendering the cockpit of all 12 cars in portrait (and the Comet and Halcyon in landscape) before and after.
- **Panel gaps closed:** the hood, door and boot lines of the imported cars are carved out of their texture's thin grey lines, and they used to come out ragged: a dashed black line along the hood's edge that read as a gap where the hood meets the bumper (clearest on the Halcyon and Last Exit, and on every car made the same way). The converter now splits the mesh further along the lines themselves (the faint grey lines are a texel category of their own, with summed-area tables to find which texels of a triangle are line, and up to three levels of splitting), so a line comes out whole and as thin as the texture draws it. It costs triangles: a car's mesh is 0.7% (Night Marshal) to 18% (Vanta) bigger, 11% on average, 13% on the Halcyon (hood paint 43,862 to 54,565 triangles). The ten imported cars that have carved lines (Pulse, Vector, Kestrel, Bruiser, Copperhead, Halcyon, Blue Static, Last Exit, Vanta and Night Marshal) were converted again; the Comet, the Hornet and the traffic SUV and van have no seam to close (looked at close up) and are as they were, and nothing else about a car changed (its measurements and hit box are the same). The converter's header lists the settings every shipped car was made with, and its `rearbody` option takes the rear bumper's position from the body alone as the first cars did, so converting a model again gives the shipped mesh byte for byte, apart from the carving.
- **Signs:** every word on a sign (the exit gantries, the kilometre markers, the FINISH gantry, the billboards, the lettering along the overpasses, the fuel stop's signs and the garage's brand wall) is set by `scripts/sign_kit.gd` as a real sign is: one letter height across a sign, names against the left margin and distances against the right in columns of their own (the rows were padded with spaces before, and the figures did not line up), capitals centred on their line, and a white line round each guide panel, inset from its edge. A gantry's panels are as wide as their longest line needs, within what fits between the posts (the longest, "Granite Falls 120 km", still sits inside its panel with a margin), the exit number is on a green tab of its own under the corner of the exit panel (it was tiny green letters on a cream box), and the destination and distance of the exit sit left-aligned. The fuel stop's approach signs have FUEL against the left margin and the distance against the right, a rule, the station's name centred and the three grades with their prices in two columns (the heading used to run out past the edges of the blue panel); the price pylon's grades and prices are in two columns with its post behind the boards (it stood in front of the prices); the kiosk's name is centred on its band. Lettering has no outline (the FINISH and kilometre signs had a dark halo), and the billboards are turned a little towards the road (they faced away from it, so they were seen at a slant from the lanes). All sign text is one font size in pixels (`SignKit.PX`), so the signs share one glyph atlas.
- **Driving physics:** cars steer like real ones, and respond quickly: the front wheels turn in fast and the car starts to move over in about a quarter of a second. The wheels turn in within a frame, the nose answers within about 20 ms and the direction of travel follows the nose as fast as the tyres can push it round (the steering may lean on about 1.4 times the car's grip stat at **Standard** response), so the car starts to move over at once and a full-lock steer-and-counter-steer lane change takes about 1.2 s (it was 1.4 s) at any speed; harsh inputs at speed still give a small slide instead of an instant sideways jump. The car's side of this is `scripts/steer_model.gd`. The body rolls and pitches about the middle of the car at hub height (like real suspension), so the wheels stay inside their arches. It moves on springs and dampers set like a road car's (`Wreck.sway`: it rocks at about 1.6 Hz side to side and 1.8 Hz nose to tail, damped to a third of critical), leaning against the way the tyres push it: about 2.9 degrees for each g round a turn on an ordinary car (less on a low sports car, more on an SUV) and 1.7 degrees nose down for each g of braking, with bump stops at 3.4 and 2.3 degrees (the room the wheel arches leave; a low car's stiffer springs stop sooner). A blow rocks it too, and it overshoots and settles when a turn or a stop ends. (It used to lean into turns: the sign of the roll was the wrong way round, in the driving and in the knock after a hit.) The car turns only by rolling forward on its turned front wheels (you can see them turn), so it can't rotate or slide sideways when it's stopped, and at a crawl it needs plenty of lock to move over. The front wheels turn at a limited rate, the available lock shrinks with speed, and the tyres grip only up to a limit, so asking for too much at speed makes the car run wide. You steer it yourself the whole time: there is no lane snapping. Braking hard and driving on the shoulder cost grip. The body leans out of turns (right side up in a right-hander), and traffic, bosses and police also move over only as fast as they're rolling forward. **At low speed** the car steers like a real one too: below about 70 km/h the front wheels take about a second to go from straight to full lock at a crawl (and 0.9 s to swing from one full lock to the other, 0.4 s at 30 km/h), the tightest turn is about 5.6 m in radius, the sideways acceleration it can ask for at a crawl is limited to about 5.5 m/s² (a bit over half a g, for a standard car), and the nose and the direction of travel answer more slowly the slower you go; from 70 km/h upwards it steers exactly as before (`SteerModel.blend` is the fade between the two, and `tools/steer_low_test.gd` prints the numbers).
- **Steering:** **Stick**: a floating analog stick that appears wherever you put your finger (away from the pedals) and steers as far as you push it left or right (past its edge it follows your finger, so turning back answers at once); **Swipe** anywhere on the screen (the start point trails your finger the same way); an on-screen **Wheel** you turn with your thumb (a short 90-degree travel; let go and it runs back to centre like a real one, faster the faster you go); **Buttons** for left and right; or **Tilt** (tilt the phone; the angle you hold it at when a run starts or resumes counts as straight, 16 degrees is full lock, and **Tilt direction** flips it if it feels backwards). Every method turns the front wheels: full input asks for about the most grip the tyres have at your speed, so it feels the same at 60 and 200 km/h, and small inputs are softened for fine corrections. Tilt is read through a one-euro filter (steady when your hand is still, almost no lag when it moves) with a short look-ahead and the gyroscope's help, and every method answers a quick movement a little ahead of itself, so a decisive flick gets a decisive turn-in while slow, steady input is left exactly as it is. **Steering response** (Relaxed, Standard, Sharp) sets how hard the car leans on its grip and how strong that look-ahead is: try Sharp if it still feels slow. **Dead zones** (Settings › Controls, one slider each for tilt, stick, wheel and swipe) set how far a control has to move before the car starts to steer: bigger ignores small wobbles, smaller answers sooner, and full lock stays at the same place whatever the setting. The defaults are what the controls always used: tilt 0.9°, stick 3.5% of its travel, wheel 3%, swipe none; the control you are steering with is highlighted.
- **Feeling what the car does:** the steering control shows the car's state: a marker (above the stick, round the top of the wheel, or on a slim bar above the speedometer when you tilt) shows which way the car's nose points, so after a lane change you steer back until it is in the middle, and the ring or bar warms from white through amber to red as the tyres reach their limit. The wheel in the cockpit turns with your hand at any speed. Vibration also buzzes like a rumble strip on the verge (quicker the faster you go) and trembles faintly when the tyres are at their limit.
- **Screen:** **Portrait** or **Landscape**. The game locks to the one you choose, so turning the phone never switches it by accident.
- **Graphics:** **High** (full shadows, anti-aliasing, speed blur at the screen edges and a colour grade), **Medium** or **Low** for older phones. On the first run on a phone the game checks the frame rate and steps down a level if it's struggling.
- **Frame rate:** **60 FPS** (the default) is smooth and keeps the phone cool; **30 FPS** is the coolest; **Screen** draws a frame every time the display refreshes, which on a 90 or 120 Hz screen is smoother and runs the phone a lot hotter (twice the drawing at 120 Hz). A save from before 60 became the default is moved to it once, so a phone that was drawing 120 frames a second now draws 60; pick Screen again to undo that. The game runs at the same speed at any rate (the simulation steps by the time between frames), but a lower rate is less smooth and takes coarser steps. Whatever you choose, a menu, the garage or the pause screen that nobody has touched for a moment and a half is drawn 30 times a second (nothing moves there but a slow turn of the car) and at full rate again as soon as a finger goes down.
- **Developer tools:** **Drift mode** (every run starts with the drift tune fitted, the rare boss reward below; switched on or off it takes effect at once, in a run going on too, and a drift tune won from a boss stays), **Infinite lives**, an **FPS counter**, a **Hitch log** (a few lines in the corner while you drive, whenever the game stops for a moment: how long, and what it was doing; see No stalls), **Show hit boxes** (outlines drawn on every car), **Unlock all cars**, **Max all upgrades**, **+$100,000 cash** and **Reset progress** (tap twice; keeps your settings).
- **Rear-view mirror:** on by default, at the top of the screen. Turn it off, or set **Mirror size**, in Settings. Move it in the layout editor.
- **Pedal layout:** **Edit pedal layout** lets you drag the gas, brake, steering wheel or buttons and the mirror anywhere on screen (**MIRROR −** / **MIRROR +** resize it there). **Swap pedals** puts the gas on the left, and **Reset pedals** returns them to the bottom corners.

All sixteen are drawn in the garage designer's own modelling language and exported from it (`tools/garage/`, see Project layout): the body lofted from its sections, the greenhouse, panel lines, grille, lamps, vents, wings, mirrors and wipers, each car's own wheels and brake callipers (the "Factory" wheels in the workshop, and the car's default) and its own interior: dash and instruments, seats front and rear, door cards and steering wheel, in its own materials (the Pulse's suede and blue accents, the Vector's red-lit dials and paddles, the Hornet's dash panel in the body colour, the Comet's grey suede buckets and digital cluster, the sedan's beige leather and wood). The interior shows through the glass, and the inside of the body round it is drawn in the interior's lining colour. Near a car (22 m, 18 m for traffic) the detailed body is drawn, about 70,000 to 88,000 triangles, with 20,000 for the interior and 20,000 for the four wheels; past that a light version of about 5,000 to 7,000 whose glass is dark, and the interior is not drawn (the light body is too rough to keep it inside: `tools/interior_check.py`).

Each car also has the small things a real one of its kind has (`tools/garage/details.js`, added to every design on export): a badge on its nose (a chrome ring with a dark field and an emblem, on the grille or the bonnet), its antenna (a shark fin ahead of the rear window on the modern cars, a mast on the roof of the Kestrel, Hornet and traffic hatch, and on the Copperhead's front wing), a **third brake light** (on the roof's back edge, under a roof spoiler or inside the top of the rear window) that is a dark red lens until the car brakes and lights only then, amber side repeaters on the front wings of the European hatchbacks and the American side markers (amber at the front corners, red at the back) on the Bruiser, Night Marshal and traffic sedan, a fuel filler door over the rear wheel (the Bruiser's a round chrome cap, the Blue Static's a charging flap), a wiper on the rear window of the hatchbacks and the SUV, and on the van the split between its back doors, a door handle, hinges, red reflectors in the bumper, the third brake light high on its back and the sliding door's track. Every headlamp projector and bulb sits in a chrome bezel, and the lamps that come on with the headlights (projectors, bulbs and amber parking lamps) are dark glass by day and light up at dusk, as on a real car (`CarModel.set_night`); the running lights and tail lights stay lit. Up close the grille mesh, slats, louvred vents, carbon weave and the interiors' quilting, pleats, tartan and wood are drawn by the car shader from where the part is on the car, and fade to their average colour further off so they never shimmer (`pattern` in `shaders/car_paint.gdshader`). The lamps, grilles and trim laid on the body no longer tear where they wrap round a corner (the Pulse's headlamps, the traffic sedan's tail lamps): each is laid on the body's own triangles, lifted off them along the panel's normal, and its triangles are split wherever they bend over a curve, so no lamp is under the paint anywhere on any car (checked on all sixteen). A car's body is now about 61,000 to 85,000 triangles near and the same 5,000 to 7,000 far, 8% fewer than before in all, and each car has at most two surfaces more. The hit boxes are measured from the new meshes and are as exact as before.

### How a run works

- **Lives:** 3. Touching a car, the rival or the guardrail costs one. The Spare chassis upgrade adds more.
- **Scoring:** points appear in a short list just under the score counter, clear of the road and of the rear-view mirror: small and white for a few points, and bigger and warmer as the amount climbs (13 px for a handful, 22 px at 1,000, 27 px at 10,000, turning from white through amber to orange). A pass is worth more the faster you go. Passing within 1 m is a **near miss** and within 35 cm is a **razor pass**. A lane change right beside a car is a **close cut**. Each of those adds to a combo multiplier (up to x8) that resets after 3.5 s without one. Above 120 km/h you also earn points every second.
- **Stages:** fill either the stage's distance bar or its points bar to trigger the next encounter.
- **World map:** **Start run** opens a road-atlas map (shaded terrain with coastline and shallows, beaches, rivers, woodland, hills and snowy peaks, each region coloured by its highway's country; winding highways with interstate shields; a little lit skyline for every city, padlocked until you can reach it; compass and scale bar) of 15 cities (Port Halden, Ashford, Millbrook, Cedar Falls, Red Mesa, Dustwater, Silver Lake, Ironvale, Northgate, Granite Pass, Copper Ridge, Sunset Bay, Lakeshore, Neon Heights and Summit City) joined end to end by 14 highways (I-10 to I-80). Each highway is its own endless run through its own country (green farmland, autumn woods, desert flats, northern pines, coast road or frosted plains, with matching ground and sky colours), and each one further along raises score targets (+14% per highway) and makes its bosses harder: the bosses of the first highways are easy and those of the last are as hard as the game gets. You start with only the first highway open: beat its final boss, Last Exit (boss 4), and the highway on to the next city opens. Tap a highway to see its country, your best score on it and how hard its bosses are (EASY on the first highways up to BRUTAL on the last), then **Drive**. Locked highways are grey dashes, and the stage sign shows which highway you're on.
- **Cars on sale:** Copperhead and Night Marshal go on sale as soon as you beat their bosses (the first and second boss of any highway). **Blue Static goes on sale when you clear the fifth highway and Last Exit when you clear the eighth** (clearing a highway is beating its fourth boss): beating their bosses before that no longer unlocks them (the prize screen says which highway does), the Cars page says what to clear ("Clear highway 8, I-44, Ironvale to Northgate, to put this car up for sale"), and a notice announces the car when the highway is cleared. A save that has already cleared those highways gets the cars when it loads, and a car an older save had already unlocked stays unlocked. The rule is a `"route"` entry on the car in `scripts/data.gd` (the highway to clear, counted from 0).
- **Every car drives differently (`scripts/car_stats.gd`, `CarStats`):** the cars used to differ by a few per cent (the five cheapest were within 4% of each other on everything: 0 to 100 km/h in 5.3 to 5.5 s, 180 to 187 km/h flat out), and some of what the garage promised did nothing: the Tyres & suspension upgrade said '+4% steering speed' but the number only the autopilot read, and a bigger tank only made a fill dearer, as every car went the same 16.5 km on one. Now each car has the traits below, in `Data.CARS`, and every one of them goes into the real physics. `CarStats` works the car's numbers out from its row, the upgrades bought for it, the boons of the run and its damage; the game, the garage and `tools/roster_test.gd` all use it, so the garage says what the car does. **The Pulse is the reference car** (pull 3, agility 1, stability 1, 1,400 kg, 16.5 km) and drives exactly as it did: in six test runs with the same seeds (`--state-log`, 4,800 frames each, a fresh save for each build) the build before and this one gave identical simulation states throughout in three of them, and in the other three until the first collision with a boss rival, which now has its own weight (the Copperhead's is 1,750 kg). The traits:
  - **Acceleration and pull.** The engine pulls with `accel` m/s² from a standstill and keeps `1 - (v / top)^pull` of it at speed v (it was always a cube). A low `pull` (the V8s) is strong low down and fades early; a high one (the revvers and a turbo that wakes up late) holds on to its pull to the top. So a car can be slower to 100 km/h and quicker from 100 to 150: the Vector takes 5.8 s to 100 km/h (the Pulse 5.5) and 4.1 s from 100 to 150 (the Pulse 5.0). Drag stops every car a few per cent short of its `top`, so the garage shows the speed it really reaches (`CarStats.terminal_speed`: the Pulse's 171 km/h, not the 180 its table said).
  - **Agility** is how readily it answers the steering: the gain of the steering about the middle (`SteerModel.shape_cmd`: a small movement of the hand moves a twitchy car more and a heavy one less; a full command is the same in every car, the curve is gentle about the middle so a still hand does not set a twitchy car wandering) and how fast the wheels, the nose and the direction of travel follow it at speed. Half a push at 125 km/h moves the Pulse 0.90 m in 0.6 s, the Hornet 1.17 m, the Vector 0.80 m and the Blue Static 1.63 m.
  - **Stability** is how settled the car stays: it loses less grip as it goes faster (the old 18% at top speed is now `lift`, 3% in the Last Exit and 26% in the Bruiser and the Blue Static), keeps more of it when the brakes are on (75% in the Pulse, 88% in the Last Exit, 69% in the Bruiser) and wears off a swing from a knock sooner and catches more of it. Changing lane while braking hard at 145 km/h takes 1.40 s in the Pulse, 1.23 in the Comet, 0.98 in the Last Exit and 1.44 in the Bruiser, and a knock that swings the nose at 1 rad/s leaves it turned 1.2 degrees in the Last Exit, 2.6 in the Pulse and 4.7 in the Bruiser and the Blue Static.
  - **Weight** (`kg`, 1,400 is an ordinary car) is the car's mass in a crash (`Vehicle.mass`, used by `Wreck.impact` for the player's car and a boss rival): a heavy car loses less speed in a hit, so it takes less damage and throws the traffic harder. A rear-end hit at 8 m/s closing slows the Kestrel (1,080 kg) by 5.4 m/s and the Bruiser (1,900 kg) by 4.1, and the same knock is 38% less wear on the Bruiser than on the Kestrel. (In the game: hitting a SUV from behind took the Hornet from 35.5 to 29.6 m/s, the Pulse from 35.7 to 30.1 and the Bruiser, coming in 2 m/s faster, from 37.7 to 31.8.)
  - **Range** (`range`, km a full tank goes flat out) is now the car's own, and the tank is only what a fill costs: it burns `tank / range` litres a km, so the Vector (52 L, 21 km) goes furthest, the Hornet (40 L, 17.5 km) is the cheapest to run of the regular cars and the Kestrel (40 L, 13 km, a stop at every fuel stop) the dearest. The Garage's Cars page shows the range and the fuel line the tank.

- **Day and night:** time runs while you drive, one real minute to two game hours (a full day in 12 minutes), shown by the speedometer. Dawn brings it all back. The boss theme, *Cold Iron Street*, fades in from 50 seconds into the track and plays through each boss or police encounter, fading out over about 6 seconds when it ends.

A 10-second countdown runs on the stage sign while you keep driving and scoring (the boss's name and how good it is), with beeps for the last three seconds, and then the boss closes in from behind. The traffic around you is left exactly as it was when the boss arrives: nothing is moved or removed. Fuel stops are moved out of the way of the countdown and the race.

They are all brought to the same loudness (about -12 LUFS over the part that plays; the trims are 0 to +1.1 dB). The song for the next encounter is picked and loaded on a worker thread as soon as one begins, so starting a chase never waits for a file (starting one takes about 2 ms); if an encounter outlasts its song, the song carries on from its start point. - **Boss race:** you have 2 km to out-score the boss before the finish gantry. Bosses score exactly as you do (near misses, razor passes, close cuts, combos and speed) and drive hard to get them. They drive the way a good player does (`CarDriver`, below): they look ahead, plan a line through the traffic, move over in one smooth curve about as long as a player's lane change (a second or so, starting and ending gently, with a limit on how hard they can move sideways), speed up and brake without jolts, and never touch a car (the old driver darted sideways in one frame and drove through cars). What grows with a boss's skill is how much it dares: the best leave 30 cm beside the car they pass and 2.5 m behind the one they follow, move over and brake hard (up to 14 m/s² sideways, 11 m/s² along the road), keep their speed up and go out of their way for the points that passing close earns (in the same traffic a legend makes about 9 razor passes a race and a rookie none, and its average speed is 5 to 6 m/s higher); the rookies leave a metre and more, keep seven metres back and mostly drive round the traffic. Each boss is better at it than the last, and so is each highway's set of bosses (`Data.boss_skill`: a rank that climbs with the highway and from boss to boss, and every lap of the four bosses adds more), **and no boss is a rookie any more** (the floor, below). How much a boss's race is worth is set apart from how it drives. `Data.boss_par` is the score a boss is meant to make over the 2 km: 3,800 for the first boss of I-10 and 8,500 for the last (they were about 1,600 and 5,100), 5,500 and 10,000 on I-14, 7,500 and 12,500 on I-27, 8,500 and 13,500 on I-30, 9,500 and 15,000 on I-40, 12,500 and 25,000 on I-60 and 19,000 and 35,000 on I-80, with the highways between them in step, the four bosses of a highway stepping evenly from the first to the last, and each lap of the four after the first 1.3 times as much (for up to four laps). From I-40 on these are about what the bosses made before. `Data.boss_yield` is what a boss's skill and car earn when its driving is scored at face value: 7,200 points times e^(0.745 × skill), times 1.0, 1.165, 1.083 or 1.073 for the Copperhead, the Night Marshal, the Blue Static or the Last Exit (fitted to 8 races at every highway and stage with `--boss-edge=1.0`, to within 13% of the mean of each set of 8; a single race varies by 40% and more). And `Data.boss_edge`, how generously its driving is scored, is the first over the second: 0.38 for the first boss of I-10, 0.68 for its last and 1.23 for the last of I-80 (it was 0.62 on I-10 and 1.25 on I-80 and did not change from boss to boss). So how bold a boss is and what its race comes to can be set one apart from the other: a bolder driver is not a higher score by itself. The new driver earns less than the old one did for the same skill (it cannot drive through cars, which the old one did for hundreds of frames in a race), so `Data.boss_style(skill)` scales what a boss's driving is worth up to the old targets: 2.7 times for the first boss of I-10, 2.2 to 2.3 times through the middle of the map and 1.75 for the hardest (it was 6 times before the driver learned to weave: the best bosses now earn most of their score by passing close). The pop-ups over the boss still show a pass at its own value; the rest of it comes into the rival's score over the next second or two (`run.b_drip`), and with the speed bonus. Win and you get prize money, choose 1 of 3 upgrades for the rest of the run, and the boss's car goes on sale in the Garage. Lose and it costs a life, followed by a rematch.
- **Drift tune (a rare boss reward, `scripts/drift_model.gd`, `DriftModel`, `tools/drift_test.gd`):** about one boss in eight (`Data.RARE_CHANCE`), one of the cards after it is a gold **★ RARE** one: the drift tune. Take it and for the rest of the run your car is a rear-wheel-drive drift car, driven the way a real one is, and **every point counts 100 times** in the run's score (`Data.DRIFT_SCORE`; not in the run's money, which goes by the points without it, nor in a stage's points target or a boss race, so it makes the score a hundred times bigger, not the bank or the bosses easier). The car is a single-track model with a tyre at each axle. The front tyres' sideways force follows their slip angle on a Pacejka curve (it builds quickly, peaks at about 12 degrees and falls to about 80% of that in a full slide). The rear tyres use **combined slip**: how fast the driven wheels spin against the road and how far the tail is sliding sideways make one slip, and the tyre's force points against it, so power, grip and slide blend into one another smoothly (a little wheelspin costs a little sideways grip, a lot lets the tail come round, easing off gives it back) instead of switching between "gripping" and "spinning". The rear wheels have a speed of their own (they spin up under power and slow under the brakes and the road), worked out implicitly so it is steady at any speed, and the engine's pull fades near the top speed, which is the rev limiter. The weight moves between the axles on the springs (over a tenth of a second) as the car speeds up and slows down, and the throttle opens over a fifth of a second. The tune gives it a 50-degree steering lock, more drive at the rear and drift tyres on the back that are stiff while they grip (so it runs straight and true at speed: a nudge at 40 m/s dies away, a lane change at 35 m/s stays on the tyres' grip) but let go sooner. The steering is speed-sensitive while the tyres grip and opens up to the full lock as the car slides. **Controls:** your usual steering (without the quick-flick boost the ordinary steering adds, which overshoots in a slide), the gas pedal, and the brake pedal, which is the **handbrake** while you throw the car in (steering at speed and not yet well into a slide: it locks the rear wheels) and the **footbrake** otherwise, mostly on the front wheels, so braking in a drift moves the weight forward and the angle grows a little, as in a real car, instead of snapping it round; the steering assist setting does not apply. **The drift assist** works like CarX Drift Racing's: as the tail slides it counter-steers for you (it points the front wheels where the front of the car is going, and your steering is added to that), it eases off the power as the angle nears its limit, looking at where the angle is heading as well as where it is, so that **holding GAS holds a steady drift** (thrown in with the handbrake and left to the assist, the car holds 41 to 44 degrees), it leans on the car progressively past 46 degrees and damps the swing carrying it further (it used to snap it back hard at 50), it steadies the car as a slide ends, and while you run straight it keeps the rear wheels from spinning up (steer hard, slide or pull the handbrake and the power is all yours). A drift on a straight road is never a steady thing (a car sliding sideways is always turning), so you drive it as a weave: throw it one way, hold it on the throttle while steering its line down the road, flick it into the other. **Points:** while the car slides at more than 12 degrees and 12 m/s it earns (speed times the sine of the angle: 3 base points a second at 1 m/s fully sideways, so a 3-second slide at 30 degrees and 100 km/h is worth about 120 before the combo and the x100), slides linked by less than 0.6 s of straight are one drift, and when it ends it is banked as DRIFT, GREAT DRIFT or EPIC DRIFT with its best angle and a link in the combo for every 1.2 s (up to three); a crash before then loses it (DRIFT LOST). **The tyres' sound** (`EngineAudio._make_squeal`, `_make_scrub`, `set_squeal`) is no longer a whistle of steady tones: the screech is a train of irregular stick-slip clicks (about 650 a second, each a different size) and a little hiss rung through three resonances around 1, 1.7 and 2.6 kHz whose pitches drift and shake, with a random chatter on the loudness; it is loudest at the edge of the tyres' grip, and as they go fully sideways or spin up a low scrub roar (rubber dragged over grit, about 120 to 900 Hz) takes over. Both grow louder and higher with speed and start at a random point in their loops. The HUD shows "DRIFT TUNE · x100" under the multiplier and, while sliding, the angle and what the drift is worth so far; the rear tyres smoke, lay black marks and squeal as hard as they slide. `tools/drift_test.gd` (17 checks) drives the car with scripted hands and feet: straight and true at speed, a lane change without sliding, a handbrake flick into a drift, the throttle holding it while a driver keeps it down the road, lifting off bringing it back straight within two seconds, a flick from one drift into the other, a spin without the assist, a steady drift on GAS alone (20 to 50 degrees and steady within 8), no yaw jolt over 6 rad/s², and braking in a drift never snapping the car round. `--drift-tune` starts runs with it fitted; `--drift-drive` (with `--autotest`) has the test driver weave down the road in drifts; `--open=pick_demo:rare` shows the pick screen with the rare card.

- **How the computer drives (`scripts/car_driver.gd`, `CarDriver`):** the boss rival and the police car are driven by one planner that behaves like a good player instead of a script that slides sideways. Each round (about three a second for a boss, a dozen lines one frame at a time so no frame pays for a whole round) it tries a handful of lines across the road (the lanes, the line it is on, and a line alongside each of the nearest slower cars at the clearance it dares), plays each forward for 4 to 7 s against where the other cars will be, and marks it: the road it makes, the points it would score passing close (for a boss that wants them), a penalty for the hardest braking along it, what it costs to move over, and a penalty for leaving less than its margin beside a car. A line that would hit a car is out; it keeps the line it is on unless another is clearly better, and does not change its mind again at once. Three things make the plan match what happens. A car that has signalled is where it is until its signal runs out and then drifts over at the speed the traffic does (so it is not in the lane it is moving into until it gets there: reserving the whole sweep from the moment it signalled made the boss stand on the brakes for cars that were still a second from its lane). The move it picks starts when the round is over, a dozen frames after the state it was planned from, so the line is played forward from where the curve it is on will have taken it by then. And every frame, if it is moving over, it checks that it could still stop sideways short of the side of any car it will be alongside, and if not it begins a steady sideways stop at once and plans again (the old last-resort guard put it back where it was, which showed as a jump). It follows its line along a quintic curve (a minimum-jerk move: it starts and ends with no sideways acceleration, so deciding again halfway does not jolt it) as long as a player's lane change (3.7 m in about 1.05 to 1.4 s), made slower when it would ask more than the car has to give sideways (the curve's coefficients and its duration are now the same pair: they were not when the car's limit could not be met, which snapped it at the end of a move). Along the road it speeds up like the intelligent driver model, and closes on a slower car with one steady squeeze of the brake (the constant deceleration that takes the speed off by the time it is the gap it keeps, begun at 2.2 to 4.2 m/s² by how much it dares, with room for what it did not see coming) instead of a ramp up to standing on the pedal, easing onto the brake over a fifth of a second unless it has to hurry. How much it dares comes from one number, `CarDriver.set_risk` (a boss's is `0.12 + 0.55 × skill`, the police car's is 1 with its own settings): the sideways acceleration it will use (9 to 14 m/s²), the quickest lane change (1.4 to 1.05 s), how hard it speeds up (5.5 to 8.5) and brakes (8 to 11 m/s²), the clearance it aims for (1.1 m to 30 cm) and the least it accepts (40 to 14 cm: the clearance it aims for used to be less than the least it would plan with, so the boldest bosses never took the close lines at all), the gap it follows at, how quickly it plans and how little better another line must be before it moves, and how much the points pull it. A boss comes in no faster than a driver of its skill would and than it can brake from for the traffic ahead (at the start of the race and when it rejoins, it takes the lane with the most open road), a boss behind you speeds up to catch up only as far as its skill allows (a rookie used to rocket through the traffic at 60 m/s and then stand on the brakes), and traffic leaves more room before it changes lane in front of a rival that is coming up fast (24 m, plus 1.2 m for each m/s the rival is faster: a car used to pull in 24 m ahead of a boss closing at 15 m/s). Two faults in the game itself showed up in these races and are mended: a car coming up behind a slow player was put on the road wherever the rubber-band spawn chose, including on top of the boss (an overlap of up to two seconds), and a newly spawned car had no reach (its rear end read as the start of the road) for its first frame. Measured in 107 boss races at the real stage 4 of five highways and the first stage of I-10 (traffic as thick as the game makes it), the boss was inside a car in one of them, for 15 frames; it braked harder than 8 m/s² in 7 to 12% of the frames (about 15% with the first driver; what is left is mostly cars cutting in and wrecks across the road); the last-resort guard that holds it behind a car stepped in for about 12 frames a race. It costs about 0.1 ms a frame on a desktop CPU (about 1 ms in the worst frame). `tools/driver_test.gd` (`godot --headless --path . --script tools/driver_test.gd`, 127 checks) drives one car through stand-in traffic and checks that it never touches a car, that its sideways acceleration and jerk stay inside bounds with no jumps, that it goes round a stopped car, through a gap in a wall of cars, and stops short of a wall with none, that it steers clear of a car that has run into its side, and that in dense traffic it keeps the speed up. With `DRIVER_BENCH=1` (or `light` or `dense`, and `DRIVER_SEEDS=N`) it is a benchmark: the game's traffic mix (cars, vans, lorries, the game's lane changes), the driver at four levels of risk for a minute, over several seeds: mean speed, the share of frames braking harder than 6 and 8 m/s², lane changes, razor and near passes, what those passes are worth, overlaps and guard events. The finished driver (8 seeds, a light road and a dense one): mean speed 33.7, 35.3, 36.8 and 40.0 m/s (light) and 30.8, 33.0, 35.4 and 35.8 (dense) from the most careful level to the boldest; points from passing 14, 60, 72 and 156 a second (light) and 12, 56, 89 and 134 (dense); the boldest makes 12 to 13 razor passes a minute and the careful none; no overlaps. - **Back gestures:** swipe in from the left or right edge of the screen to go back one step in any menu (settings, garage, map, help, game-over, replay, refuel), the same as the Android back button. While driving it does nothing, so it can't pause by accident.
- **Impact feel:** every knock rocks the body on its springs away from the blow (a damped spring that settles in half a second), and hard hits freeze time for a moment.
- **Hit boxes:** a car's hit box is its real outline seen from above, made from its model by `tools/make_hulls.py`: the whole body below the side windows (bumpers, wheel-arch flares, lamps, plates, the Night Marshal's push bar, high rear haunches and wing end plates) and the tyres, as a convex outline of up to 28 corners whose edges are pushed out until all of the car is inside, and the door mirrors as small pieces of their own (they touch other cars but stand above the guardrails, so the rails meet the body). Measured against each car's footprint on a 1 cm grid, no hit box leaves out more than a centimetre of its car, and the only room it takes in is the hollow of the doors between the wheel-arch flares (1 to 3% of the car's area). The outline is pulled in only 1.5 cm (it was 5 cm), so a touch is a touch of the paint; the lorry is two pieces, box and cab. A tapering nose, rounded tail corners and wheel-arch flares count and the empty corners of a rectangle don't. The outline is turned the way the car points, which also decides where it meets the guardrail (a nose turned towards it touches first) and gives the gap of a near miss. Contact is found by the separating-axis test, which also works out where the two cars touched. **Show hit boxes** in Settings > Developer (or `--hitboxes`) draws the outlines.
- **Knocks:** a blow between two cars is worked out the way it really is: an impulse at the point where the outlines touch, along the line of the contact, shared out by weight and by how far the point is from the middle of each car. How much they bounce apart depends on how hard it is, as measured in real crashes (`Wreck.bounce`): a bumper tap at walking pace springs back with about 0.35 of the closing speed, a 5 m/s knock with 0.16 and a crash at speed crumples the metal and keeps about 0.05; along the surface the paint scrapes past at a glancing touch (friction 0.3) and the panels dig into each other in a hard hit (up to 0.6, `Wreck.contact_mu`). A square rear-end shove only shoves (you lose about the speed difference, the other car gains it, nobody spins); a hit at the corner spins the car it hits and swings your nose (the tyres wear that swing off, you steer back); a side swipe pushes both apart and drags the slower car along. Lorries are 3.5 times as heavy, vans 1.5 and SUVs 1.25. A car that is really hit is a wreck from then on: it is no longer driven, slides, spins and comes to rest by itself, and if it slides into other cars or the guardrail they take the blow too; a light tap (under 1.5 m/s of change of speed and hardly turned, `Game.LIGHT_TAP`) leaves its driver in control: it is nudged, lifts off, eases back to the middle of its lane and drives on. Your car against the guardrail is the same: the corner that touches takes the blow (`Game._rail_impact`), so a glancing scrape costs little speed and the beam springs you back off it, an angled hit costs a lot and swings the nose back towards the road (or the tail, if it touched first). Hard knocks throw off bits of bodywork, trim and glass.
- **Wrecks:** a wrecked car is a rigid body on the road, seen from above (`scripts/wreck.gd`). Its four tyres rub on the road: sideways they hold about 1 g however fast it slides, along their own direction they only drag (rolling resistance and what is left of the brakes), and each patch of rubber is pushed by the car's own movement at that corner, so a car sliding sideways scrubs speed off at about 9 m/s², a spin on the spot dies in a fraction of a turn, a car that is only slightly crooked straightens up, and a fast one that is turned right round slides on backwards, all without anything being faked. Each tyre grips by the weight on it, and the weight moves as in a real car with its centre of gravity above the road (0.55 m for a car, 0.72 for an SUV, 0.92 for a van, 1.3 for a lorry: `Wreck.CG_H`): braking hard puts about two thirds of it on the front wheels, sliding sideways about four fifths on the outer ones, and a tyre pressed harder grips a little less for each kilo (`Wreck.wheel_loads`); the air pushes back too, three to four times harder side-on than nose-on. No car spins faster than about 3 rad/s (a turn in two seconds). The wheels stay on the road and the body moves on its springs above them (the same springs as when the car is driven): the tyres push the car at road level, below its centre of gravity, so a car sliding sideways dips on the side it is sliding towards, a braking one dips its nose, and one sliding backwards dips its tail; when the slide stops the body rocks back past level and settles. A blow rocks it as well: it is worked out at the height it lands (the bumpers and sills at about 0.46 m for a car, 0.55 for an SUV, 0.75 for a lorry's, the guardrail's beam at 0.55: `Wreck.kick`), shared between the body and the wheels hanging under it, so a car hit below its centre of gravity first swings its top towards the blow, then leans away as its tyres hold it back; a shunt dips the nose of the car behind and lifts the one in front's. Wrecks lie where they stop, so a car can end up across a lane. `tools/crash_test.gd` checks all of it (280 checks): the bounce of a tap and of a crash, where the weight goes, momentum kept and energy lost in every blow, a car with locked rear wheels spinning round from a small swing (199 degrees, sliding backwards) where a rolling one runs straight, which way and how far the body leans, rocks and settles, that no car's tyres can roll it, 120 random slides, spins and blows that never take a body past its bump stops, and 300 random crashes.
- **Last-life crash:** losing your final life is the same physics with nothing held back and nothing added: the crash goes on from exactly what the blow left (its speed and direction however far round that is, its spin, and the body rocking on its springs), worked out from the real contact or the corner of the car that touched the guardrail. Nobody is in control any more: the driver stands on the brakes and the rear wheels lock (`Wreck.rear_lock`), and a car with locked rear wheels has nothing to keep its tail behind it, so the slightest swing the blow gave it grows into a spin until it slides backwards, as a real one does. It spins out in slow motion on its wheels, its body leaning and rocking on its springs, leaving tyre marks and smoke, bits of bodywork, trim and glass fly off, and the wreck smokes once it stops (a crash plays out in about four to five seconds). The sliding car collides with traffic instead of passing through it: both cars are shoved, spun and rocked by each blow and the cars it hits become wrecks too, while traffic behind stops or goes round. It never flips: on a flat road a car's tyres hold about 0.95 g sideways and it would take at least 1.27 g to lift any car in the game over its outer wheels (`Wreck.tip_g`), another car's bumper meets it near the height of its centre of gravity, and a guardrail's beam is built to catch a car and turn it along the road, not to trip it. (It used to be tripped over by the foot of the guardrail and tumble down the road.) **Look around the crash:** while it plays out, drag a finger across the screen (or the mouse) to swing the camera round the car and up or down, from just above the road to nearly overhead; let go mid-swipe and it carries on round, slowing; pinch (or turn the mouse wheel) to draw it in or back, from half to twice its distance. Left alone for a moment it carries on its own slow turn from where you left it. A hint says so for a few seconds as the crash begins.
- **Crash damage:** every knock leaves its mark on the car that took it, on the player's car, the traffic, the rival and the police alike (`scripts/car_damage.gd`). A blow becomes a dent, a point on the car's own body with the way it was pushed, how wide and how deep (a hard knock at the nose or tail also shortens that whole end, and a second knock nearby deepens the first dent instead of making another; at most eight dents per car). Each dent is pressed into a private copy of the car's meshes, so the body, the lamps and the glass crumple together with a ripple in the metal: the surface normals tilt into the dent so the reflections bend round it and the paint scuffs dark. The work is done on the engine's worker threads, a few thousand vertices at a time in parallel and with every dent that is waiting pressed in together in one pass, so a pile-up never makes a long frame: the dents appear a moment after the blow, like a crumple. The main thread only starts a batch and, when it is done, shares what is left out a mesh a frame: a private copy of one of the car's meshes (made from the arrays the kit's mesh was made from, which are kept for this: reading a mesh back from the graphics card makes everything wait until the card has caught up), and putting the pressed vertices into one mesh, which is only done to a mesh a dent of the batch touched, and a mesh is not uploaded at all until its first dent is in it (measured on a desktop CPU, a phone will be slower: the longest frame of a crash is 5 to 8 ms; the first hit on a car used to stop the game for 25 to 40 ms, and every batch landing on a car after it for 9 to 13 ms; see No stalls). The glass nearest the blow cracks (from a knock of 4 m/s, more with a harder one; the cracked window is a still-transparent web of cracks with a light haze, drawn by `shaders/car_glass_cracked.gdshader` in a copy of the glass that only that car has, so every other car's glass stays the one plain shared material), lamps at the nose or tail go dull when it is 5 or more, the number plate is lost from 6.5, and a knock of 9 or more at a corner of the nose or tail tears the wheel off about half the time: it bounces and rolls away down the road and the car drops on that corner. A car that has taken a lot, or a hit of 8 or more, smokes from its engine, and other cars that are badly wrecked catch fire. Your own view stays clear: the glass over your cockpit cracks at most half way and your car never burns. The garage can show it: `--dent=front:9,left:6`.

- **Tutorial:** the first time the game is opened a welcome card offers it (**START THE TUTORIAL**, or **SKIP · I KNOW HOW TO PLAY**); it is on the main menu (**TUTORIAL**) after that. A green sign at the top of the screen gives one lesson at a time and ticks it off as you do it: **accelerate** to 100 km/h (the gas pedal pulses), **steer** into another lane and back (the words follow your steering method), **brake** below 60 km/h (the brake pedal pulses), then traffic appears: **weave through** it past 5 cars, get 2 **near misses** (and see the multiplier climb), and **how a run works** (lives, bosses, fuel stops). - **Damage stays until it is repaired:** the car you drive keeps what crashes do to it, from one run to the next, until you pay for it at the garage, and the worse it is the worse the car drives. Every knock adds **wear** (0 as new, 1 wrecked) to the car that took it: `(strength - 2) / 10` of 0.15, so a tap adds next to nothing and a hard knock (12 m/s or more) a seventh of the car's life, and one crash (the knocks within 3 s of each other) adds at most a quarter (`Data.wear_for_strength`). Wear takes performance off straight away, in the run it happens in and in every run after (`Data.damage_effects`), in proportion to the wear: at full wear a car has 40% less acceleration, 35% less braking, a quarter less top speed, a fifth less grip and a tenth less sideways grip at a crawl, and uses a fifth more fuel (at 60% that is 24%, 21%, 15%, 12% and 6% less, and 12% more fuel). Each car has its own record in the save file (`damage`, then the car's id: its `wear`, its `dents` as numbers, the cracked glass, whether the lamps are out and the plate is gone, and which wheels are lost; `CarModel.damage_state` writes it and `restore_damage` reads it) and puts it back when the car is built, in the garage and at the start of a run: `CarDamage.restore` presses the saved dents in on the worker threads (so they show a moment after the car does), the cracks, dull lamps and missing plate are put back at once, a lost wheel stays off and its corner sags, and a car that is 60% worn or more smokes from the start. The record is written to the save about a second after a crash settles, whenever the app is paused or closed, and at the end of the run, so losing the app mid-run does not undo it (the run's earnings are still only paid at its end); a record that cannot be read (a number that is not a number, a wheel that does not exist) is skipped a part at a time and cannot break the car. The Workshop's **Repair** tab (Garage, Workshop, Repair) shows the wear, what it costs the car and the price, and the Repair button takes the money and clears the record: $40, plus $150 and 1.2% of the car's price in proportion to the wear, rounded to $10 (from $80 for a Pulse at 30% to $550 for a wrecked Last Exit; `Data.repair_cost`); with too little cash the button says how much more is needed. **Once a car is more than 10% worn a red REPAIR CAR button with the price is always there:** on the garage's main screen under START RUN (it opens nothing: it pays, and the car on the turntable comes back mended with a flash of light), on the run-over screen (with a note of how worn the car is and what it costs), and on the Workshop's Repair tab, which shows the price in red; with too little cash it says how much more is needed and cannot be pressed. A damaged car says so: the main menu's car card ('DAMAGED, 80% WORN') with its stats as they are now, the Cars page ('DAMAGED 80%'), a banner at the start of a run and a note at the end of one. **A car at 100% is a wreck:** the instant its wear reaches 1 (`Data.WRECKED_AT`, the garage shows 100%) the run ends, whatever lives are left (the car is shown crashing as it does when your last life goes, then **CAR WRECKED**), and it cannot be driven again until it has been repaired: START RUN becomes **REPAIR YOUR CAR**, which opens the Workshop's Repair tab (with `WRECKED · 100% · REPAIR IT TO DRIVE` on the main menu's damage line and on the world map's drive button), where the Repair button comes first; or pick another car you own. At 75% and at 90% a banner warns that the car is worn out, and a run that starts with a car at 70% or more says so. A wreck that is the only car you own is mended for what you have (so a player with no money and no other car cannot be left with a car they can neither drive nor afford to mend; `Save.repair_price`). `--godmode` is exempt, and `--autotest` repairs a wreck by itself at the start of a run. `--wear=F` gives the car you drive that wear, `--hold-over` keeps the run-over screen up. Repairs fix the car selected in the Workshop only, and only the car you drive keeps damage: the traffic, rival and police cars you hit are new next time. `--autotest` logs it: `run starts with a 25% worn pulse: top speed 169 km/h, acceleration 4.86; pull 3.0, agility 1.00, stability 1.00, 1400 kg, range 16.5 km` and `player's car: knock of 7.9 m/s, wear now 0.338, 2 dents, top speed 165 km/h`.
- **Notifications:** the police, a boss, a new stage and the rest are slim bands that slide in from the right edge of the screen, stay a moment and slide out again (a red stripe for alerts, green for good news, amber for information), instead of a big banner across the middle of the road.
- **Scoring sound:** every score that counts towards the combo (a near miss, a razor pass, a close cut, a drift) is a **whoosh** of air past the car: a band of noise whose pitch sweeps up from about 770 Hz to 2.9 kHz as it comes and back down to 600 Hz as it goes (two resonant band-pass filters one after the other, the top taken off above 4 kHz), over a low rush of air and a faint falling tone, half a second long; a big score (a razor pass, a long drift) is played deeper and louder. When the score's **multiplier** goes up a whole number (x2, x3 ... x8) a bright **ping** rings out (a glassy strike at G6 with a quieter shimmer above it), a step higher up a major scale for each, and the multiplier's badge jumps. The drift tune's sliding tyres squeal (a loop of close, wavering tones over a rough hiss, louder and a little higher the harder they slide). Glass has its own breaking sound. They are made in code (`scripts/engine_audio.gd`), and `--save-sfx=DIR` writes them out as WAV files to look at.
- **Running cool:** the game does the same work with less of it, so the phone stays cooler without anything looking or driving differently. Measured without a window, the script side of a frame is about a third lighter and the simulation is bit-for-bit the same (`--state-log` over eight different runs of 80 seconds each, with police, fuel stops, other cars, other highways and heavy traffic, gives identical lines before and after). Drawn with the renderer the phone uses (on a software Vulkan driver, as nothing else was at hand), the pictures are identical to the pixel, except that the sky and the light now follow the time of day in steps and that a menu nobody is touching is drawn 30 times a second (see below). What changed:
  - **Sky and light:** they were set from the time of day every frame, which cost a fifth of the script time (and every change to the sky is work for the renderer too; only the script side could be measured here). Now each of four parts (the sun, the sky and haze, the night look of the cars, the scenery and stars) is set every fifth of a second, one part at a time. A day passes in 12 minutes, so in that time the colours move by well under one step of the 8-bit screen (compared pixel by pixel with setting them every frame: 0.2 to 0.5 of a colour level off on average).
  - **HUD:** a bar's fill, a label's colour and the stage sign's colour are set only when they change (setting one, even to the same value, made the control work out its size and shape its text again, or redraw everything that uses the style), and text that changes once in a while (the speed unit line, the clock, litres, the stage's title) is built once, not every frame.
  - **Cars:** the traffic's look-ahead only considers cars it could react to (nearer than 45 m, using plain arrays); the collision test skips cars whose outlines cannot touch; the wheels of a car are turned in one step, and the body is not moved again while a car stands still.
  - **Sound:** the engine is not synthesised at all while the master volume is muted (the garage, or sound off in Settings).
  - **Menus:** the garage, the main menu and the pause screen are drawn 30 times a second once nobody has touched them for a moment and a half (nothing moves there but a slow turn of the car), and at full rate again as soon as a finger goes down.
  - **Release build:** the debug export carries the debugger and its checks, which cost the scripts some speed (how much was not measured here, only a desktop build was at hand). See Build for Android for the release export (it is signed with the same key, so it installs over the debug one).
  - **Frame rate** in Settings (Screen, 60 or 30): on a 120 Hz screen drawing every refresh is twice the drawing of 60 a second; a limit of 60 halves that work. It was off by default at first, because it is the one thing here that does change how smooth the game looks; it is on (60) by default now.
- **Running cooler, second round:** the first round cut the script's work; this one cuts what the graphics chip is asked to draw, which is where most of a phone's heat comes from. The trees, bushes and grass tufts along the road were one big multimesh for each kind, running the whole 1,800 m the scenery covers, and the renderer can only draw such a mesh whole or not at all, so every frame it drew every tree for a kilometre behind and beyond sight, and again for the sun's shadow maps (which reach 90 m) and again for the rear-view mirror and its own shadow maps. Each kind is now cut into 150 m pieces (`Scenery.CHUNK`) so the pieces nobody can see are skipped. Measured with `--perf` on one run (portrait, High graphics, Mesa's software Vulkan, so triangles and draw calls rather than milliseconds): per frame, 4.45 million triangles became 1.79 million, 60% fewer (the main view 1.06 → 0.72 million, its shadow pass 1.50 → 0.60, the mirror 0.74 → 0.22 and its shadow pass 1.15 → 0.24), for 688 → 761 draw calls (the extra pieces cost 11% more draw calls and nothing measurable in script time). Frames from before and after the cut into pieces (by day and at dusk, at two moments of the same run) are identical to the pixel. A second, smaller saving is that the pine cones and tree trunks are built from one ring each instead of four (a cone's side is the same shape however many rings it is cut into), which takes a pine from 88 triangles to 32: frames from before and after that differ in well under 1% of their pixels, by more than 4 colour levels (of 255) on 0.05% at most, on distant trees, and never by more than 34. What was tried and left alone because it did change the picture: a 2048 shadow map instead of 4096 (shadow acne on roofs) and switching the glow off by day (a fifth of the pixels shift). `--perf` also lists the heaviest meshes in the scenery, in a traffic car and in the player's car now (the ones that cast shadows are marked), and `--shadow-atlas=N` and `--noglow` are the two switches for that comparison.
- **No stalls (`--hitch`, the Hitch log):** now and then one frame took far longer than the rest (the game stopped for a moment), and while it did the car could not be steered. `--hitch=MS` (see Test flags) prints every frame of a run that took MS milliseconds or more, with where the script's time went, what the engine spent and what the game itself noted in it (`PerfProbe.note`: a body being built, a mesh being put in, the save being written). Headless on a desktop CPU (a phone is several times slower, so each of these was worse there, and none of this has been tried on a phone) it found three causes, all of them work done at the moment it was needed, and they are fixed:
  - **A boss or a police car stopped the game for 0.2 to 0.9 s (1.3 s with nothing built yet) the first time one of its kind came along:** its body kit (`CarModel._kit`, `_mesh_kit`) was built then. Most of that was measuring the body's inner shell (what keeps the cabin inside the bodywork: reading the whole mesh back from the graphics card and going over every triangle in script) and converting the mesh file. The shells are now measured once: `tools/bake_shells.tscn` writes `assets/models/car_shells.shells` (282 KB), which the game reads. A baked shell is only used for a mesh file of the size it was measured for and a car of the same size, and the game measures any other body itself, the slow way. **Run `tools/bake_shells.tscn` again after changing a car mesh (`assets/models/*.mesh`), a car's size or `CarModel._shell_of`; `tools/kit_test.tscn -- --verify` says whether the baked shells are still what measuring gives.** The mesh file is read straight into the arrays too, and a distant version that has no mesh of its own shares the near one. A kit now takes about 30 ms to build where a boss's took about 200. And nothing is built in the middle of a race any more (every one of the 4,880 frames of the police chase below was checked: no body or wheel was built in any of them, and a boss's car or the police car takes 0.6 ms to put on the road): while the garage or the menu shows, a frame builds about 3 ms of the kits a run can need (one kit or one or two wheels a frame) (`Game._warm_up_list`: the five traffic bodies, each boss's car, and the wheels of every body type in every rim style a car of it can have), and whatever is left is built when a run starts, before its first frame. `tools/kit_test.tscn` builds every kit in the order the game meets them and prints how long each took and a fingerprint of every mesh in it: the fingerprint is the same (`3bf43e60`) before and after, so not a vertex changed.
  - **A new wheel stopped it for 3 to 7 ms:** a wheel mesh was made for each size, rim style *and rim colour*, and the first traffic car with one the session had not met made its own (about one car in four early on). A mesh does not depend on the colour, so there is now one for each size and style, and a car's rim colour is the material on the rim's surface (`CarModel._rim_mat`). The cars look the same (the screenshots were compared pixel by pixel), and the wheels of all the traffic are made in the garage with the kits.
  - **A crash stopped it for 25 to 40 ms:** the first hit on a car read its meshes back from the graphics card and copied them all at once, and every batch of dents after it uploaded every mesh of the car again. Now `CarDamage` keeps the arrays and copies one mesh a frame, puts one mesh into the graphics card a frame and only if a dent touched it, and leaves a mesh out of the card until its first dent is in it. `tools/damage_test.tscn` presses the same blows into three cars, steps the damage as the game does and prints the longest frame's share and a fingerprint of the vertices, which is identical to what it was.
  - **Measured (headless, the same seed, before and after):** a police chase on the second stage, 4,880 frames (`--cop --start-stage=2 --hitch=8`): the worst frame 208 ms (a boss's body being built) → 13 ms, frames of 8 ms or more 18 → 9 (none of them building anything), the 99th percentile 6.4 → 5.1 ms. A hard rear-end crash on an empty road, 2,880 frames (`--target-scale=1000 --collide-at=14:rear:6 --hitch=6`): the worst frame 39 → 8 ms, frames of 6 ms or more 27 → 7, the 99th percentile 5.9 → 3.8 ms.
  - **What that cannot see, and the Hitch log:** a graphics card compiles a shader the first time something that uses it is drawn (the first smoke or sparks, the first LED sign in sight), and a phone's driver can stop a frame for that for longer than anything above. A renderer with no graphics card, all there was here, cannot show it. **Settings › Dev › Hitch log** puts three lines in the corner while you drive whenever a frame, from one to the next, took 45 ms or more (more with the 30 FPS limit): how long it took, what the game noted in it (a car being built, a mesh being put in) or "none of the game's work: the engine, graphics or phone", how many so far, and the worst. A hitch with nothing noted in it is not something the game built, and what was on the screen then (a crash, a boss coming, a sign) is the lead.
- **A world that looks like where it is (`scripts/flora.gd`, `Flora`; `scripts/scenery.gd`; `shaders/grass.gdshader`, `sea.gdshader`):** every highway looked the same, and not like any country: the same cartoon trees stood on a desert and on snow, the ground was a flat sheet with stripes across it, and the trees and bushes were pale mint. What changed:
  - **The colours were wrong (a bug).** The trees' colours were put in the vertices as sRGB, but the material read them as linear, which makes a dark green several times too bright: that was the washed-out, frosted look. `Flora.material` says the colours are sRGB (`vertex_color_is_srgb`), and the greens are now green.
  - **Each kind of country has its own plants** (`Data.BIOMES` "flora": the 14 highways run through six kinds). **Farmland** (I-10, I-14, I-80): oaks, rows of poplars, hedges along the fields, a few spruces. **Autumn woods** (I-21, I-40): the same shapes in orange, red and gold on ordinary bark, rust-coloured fields. **Northern pines** (I-44, I-55): a wall of spruce close to the road, ferns, mossy ground. **Desert** (I-27, I-30, I-64): saguaros, barrel cacti, prickly pears, agaves, sagebrush, a dead tree or two, red rock, mesas on the horizon. **Coast** (I-35, I-70): palms, dune grass, a beach, and the sea on the left. **Frosted plains** (I-60, I-77): spruces under snow, bare trees, white mounds for bushes, snow ground with bare patches. Every plant is a small mesh (30 to 290 triangles) with its colours baked into the vertices: a crown lighter on top and darker underneath (its normals lean to the sky, so the underside is lit and not black), a blade of grass dark at the root and bright at the tip, a spruce white on the upper side of each tier under snow. The old tufts of grass were cones, and showed as white pyramids.
  - **The ground rolls.** Beside the road, from about 18 m out, the ground swells by a few metres (`Scenery.swell_h`, raised by each country's `Flora.RELIEF`: 0.8 on the coast, 1.6 among the pines), and the hills begin about 78 m out (`Scenery.hill_h`); the plants and the rocks stand on that ground (`cfg.ground`). The swells and hills are drawn with the ground's own shader, so their colour carries on from the fields without a seam.
  - **The ground has the country's own look** (`grass.gdshader`, `style` 0 to 4 in `Data.BIOMES`): a gravel edge along the pavement and kept verge in soft drifts along the road (they were bands across it); then, by country, a patchwork of fields about 35 m by 95 m (pasture, crop with rows, stubble, ploughed soil) with a hedge line round each, desert soil in broad patches with scrub and stones, a forest floor of needles and moss that darkens under the canopy, dunes with dune grass and a wet beach by the sea, or snow in drifts with bare ground showing through. Detail fades with the size of a pixel on the ground, so it never shimmers.
  - **The horizon and the air.** Each country has its own two ranges (`Scenery.FAR`): terracotta mesas in the desert, jagged snowy peaks on the plains, dark ridges among the pines, low hazy hills on the coast. By day the haze takes a tint of the country (`Data.BIOMES` "fog"): warm dust, cool pine mist, bright sea air. On a coast road the left of the highway is a beach and then the sea (`sea.gdshader`: shallows turning deep blue, swells that tilt the surface so the sun glints on it and it mirrors the sky, crests, foam on the shore).
  - **The highway itself.** The edge line on the median side of each carriageway is yellow, as on a real divided highway, and the outer edge white (`median_side` in `road.gdshader`). **Speed limit signs** stand by the right-hand rail every 2.6 km, in the units of the speedometer (120 km/h or 75 mph: the limit the speed checks enforce, which no sign told you about before; `Scenery._setup_speed_sign`).
  - **It costs less, not more.** The plants are drawn in pieces 150 m long, and a piece beyond where the depth fog has hidden its plants is not drawn at all (a range for each kind of plant: tufts 170 m, bushes 300 m, trees 420 m); the boulders, which were one mesh 1,800 m long that was always drawn, are in pieces too, and hills and rocks are made once and used three times. Measured with `--perf` under a window (the same seed and frame): the first highway went from 1,335,000 triangles drawn in all views to 1,039,000 and the desert to 935,000. The scenery now takes 157 ms to build at start (it took 312), and the plants of a country are made when its highway is chosen on the map (`Game._warm`), 13 to 15 ms each, and kept.
  - **Tests and tools.** `tools/flora_test.tscn` (344 checks): every plant has a sane mesh, size and colours; each country builds, builds the same twice, stays within a budget of plants and triangles, puts nothing on a fuel stop or a speed check (it found plants that had been jittered into one) and stands every plant on the ground; the ground functions repeat every 600 m and agree on both sides of the road; every highway's landscape has what the ground shader and the horizon need. `tools/flora_gallery.tscn` shows every plant under the game's sun (`-- --shot=PATH --keys=oak0,saguaro0 --cols=5 --cam=x,y,z,tx,ty,tz`). `--route=N --hour=H` shows any highway at any time of day. The simulation is unchanged: with the same seed a run plays the same, frame for frame, until the first knock (wear is added by wall-clock time, `Game._wear_t`, so a test run's later crashes can differ by a little).
- **Typefaces and the lettering on the cars (`scripts/fonts.gd`, `Fonts`; `scripts/lettering.gd`, `Lettering`; `scripts/car_lettering.gd`, `CarLettering`; `scripts/tail_frames.gd`, `TailFrames`):**
  - **The game's own typefaces are bundled** (`assets/fonts`, all SIL Open Font Licence, the licences in `assets/fonts/licenses`). The game used to ask the phone for "Impact" or "sans-serif" and got whatever the phone had. Titles, buttons and the score are now **Chakra Petch Bold**, an angular face with cut corners; sentences Chakra Petch SemiBold; small labels and the figures that change (speed, distance, money, the cockpit's displays) **Oxanium ExtraBold**, angular too and with figures all the same width, so a number does not shiver as it counts. **The road signs are unchanged**: SignKit letters them in the engine's own font, as before.
  - **Every car's name is real lettering in a typeface of its own**, not text printed over the paint: the outlines of the font's letters are made into solid 3D letters (`Lettering.word`: curves cut into short pieces, holes cut out, triangulated by `Geometry2D`) that stand a few millimetres off the tail, with a chamfer along their front edge that catches the light and a dark groove round every letter, as if set into the panel. The typeface and finish suit the car (`CarLettering.BADGES`): Pulse in Russo One chrome; VECTOR in slanted Chakra Petch chrome with a yellow GS (its callipers are yellow); HORNET in black Racing Sans on the yellow paint; *Comet* in a chrome script; KESTREL in thin, widely spaced gunmetal Oxanium, almost flush; VANTA in Orbitron chrome with a red GT; BRUISER in a chunky chrome slab serif (Alfa Slab); HALCYON in wide gunmetal Michroma with a yellow R; COPPERHEAD in a chrome stencil serif; MARSHAL in chrome Roman capitals (Cinzel), flush; BLUE STATIC lit white (Audiowide); LAST EXIT in gold stencil (Saira Stencil); POLICE in flat blue. The traffic's ten makes have a typeface each too (`CarLettering.MAKES`: ARCO, NOVA, CIVITA, ...), and the seven hauliers' names along the trailers are 3D lettering in a face each (`CarLettering.FLEETS`, made smaller if a wide face would run off the trailer).
  - **The letters lie on the tail as that body's tail lies.** `tools/bake_tails.tscn` reads every body's own triangles and writes `scripts/tail_frames.gd`: where the panel is at the height of the name, how it leans and how it curves from side to side (the lettering is bent to the curve). The old labels hung a fixed 2 cm off the origin of the body: up to 5 cm proud of a tail that sits further in (Last Exit's), and on the Halcyon, Blue Static and Last Exit they sat on the lamp bar; those three now have their name between the lamp bar and the plate, and their plate over the pocket modelled for it (it hung above the pocket, so the car had two plates). Bodies with no height of their own put the name just above the plate. A crash presses the letters in with the panel (they are among the meshes a dent moves), so they do not hang in the air over a dent.
  - **Number plates:** the cars of the garage, the bosses and the police have plates whose characters stand off the plate (Staatliches, a tall narrow face, in dark blue). The traffic's plates are new for every car, so they stay printed (a Label3D), in the same face.
  - **Cost:** a name is 500 to 6,000 triangles and 1 to 12 ms to build on a desktop (three to five times as long on a phone), so every one is built before a run, in the garage's warm-up list, and kept: traffic cars of a make share one mesh. A name shows within 32 m (beyond, a letter is under two pixels high); a haulier's name within 90 m. `tools/lettering_test.tscn` (697 checks): every typeface has every character it is asked for, every name, plate and haulier's name is a sound mesh within its triangle budget and build time, is built once and kept, lies on its tail within 4 mm, is clear of the tail lamps, and `TailFrames` still fits the bodies (it fails if a body changes and the frames are not baked again). `--inspect=ID:YAW:PITCH:DIST:FOV:x:tail` looks at the name on a car's tail, and `--inspect-ref=res://tools/models/car_X.glb` shows the model file itself in the same place and light, to compare it with the game's car (`ID` a car, `t_SHAPE` a traffic body, or `police`).
- **Replay editor:** the last 20 seconds are always recorded. Open it with **Watch replay** after a run or **Replay** in the pause menu. Play, pause, step frame by frame, scrub the timeline, play at 0.25x to 2x, and switch between Chase, Hood, Cockpit, Free cam, Keyframes, Trackside and Drone cameras. **Set in** and **Set out** trim the clip, which then loops.
  - **Hide UI** (top right) gives a clean view with no controls, name tags or score messages. You can still move the camera; tap the screen or the faint **Show UI** button to bring the controls back.
  - **Free cam:** drag to swing the camera round the car, pinch to move in and out, and drag with two fingers to pan. The camera follows the car.
  - **Keyframes:** set up a view, then press **+ KEY** to keyframe it at the playhead. Move the playhead, set up another view and add another key. The **Keyframes** camera glides smoothly between them, and keys show as diamonds on the timeline. **− KEY** deletes the key under the playhead, **< KEY** / **KEY >** jump between keys, and **Clear** removes them all.
- **Fuel:** every run starts with a full tank, shown by the gauge under the speedometer (in the cockpit, on the dashboard screen). Driving flat out burns it fastest. Run dry and the car coasts to a stop and the run ends. About every 10 km there's a fuel stop on the right: big blue signs 600 m and 300 m before it (7.2 by 5.8 m) and a 16.7 m price pylon, with a lit brand-coloured header over a board 7.6 m wide, show its prices in **lit-up LED figures** (below). A **fuel stop tracker** at the top right (under the CAM and pause buttons, above where the notification bands come in) counts down the distance to where the exit lane begins, with a bar that fills as it comes up; it shows when the lane is within 3 km (or as soon as the tank is under 30%), and once the lane begins it turns green and says **MOVE RIGHT**, with the distance to the pumps, until it is too late to pull in. A kilometre before the lane begins, **FUEL STOP IN 1 KM** (with the station, your grade's price and, after the first stop of a run, how much the prices are up on the last one) comes down across the top of the screen, holds for five seconds and fades. Move right into the exit lane (from any lane, at any speed, anywhere along it from where it peels off the road) and the car pulls in and parks right beside a pump by itself: it takes the first pump it can still stop at, brakes as hard as it has to for it (up to about 2.4 g, so even a car flat out at 250 km/h that gets into the lane early enough stops at the pump), and swings across to the bay from wherever it is, never over the rail and never in a dart. A car that comes in slowly speeds up to about 50 km/h first instead of crawling to the pumps. Only a car that really cannot stop in time is left to drive on, with a "SLOW DOWN TO PULL IN" hint: brake yourself and it takes you in the moment it can. In test runs (`--pit-test`, below) a car that got into the lane 110 m before the pumps was taken in from 3 m/s up to 72 m/s (259 km/h), one 80 m before from every speed up to 55 m/s (198 km/h) and one 55 m before at up to 40 m/s (144 km/h), stopping with its middle within a centimetre of the pump every time. **Hold to pump** for as much as you want, or **Fill up**, then **Pay & go**. You pay from this run's earnings first, then your bank.
  - Each station sets its own prices. The cheaper cars (Pulse, Vector, Hornet, Comet, Kestrel, Vanta) take regular, the middle ones (Copperhead, Bruiser, Night Marshal) mid-grade, and the fastest (Halcyon, Blue Static, Last Exit) premium. At the first stop of a run regular costs $2.40 to $4.40 a litre, mid-grade about $5 more and premium about $15 more (every price ends in 9, as pump prices do).
  - **Fuel gets dearer as a run goes on:** every stop asks **5 to 25% more than the one before it**, whether or not you stopped at that one (it is the stations you go past that count). The rise is rolled for each stop (`Game._station_prices`) and is the same for all three grades; it is worked out on the prices as they are written on the sign, which move in steps of ten cents, so each grade's new price is the pump price nearest to the rise rolled and never less than 5% or more than 25% above the last (`Data.raised_price`; a $3.49 litre cannot rise by exactly 6%, so it becomes $3.69, which is 5.7%). The mean rise is 15% a stop, so regular costs about $6.70 a litre at the 6th stop of a run that began at $3.40, $13.40 at the 11th (about 110 km in), $54 at the 21st and $219 at the 31st, and a full tank costs about three and a half times as much at the 10th stop as at the first: the further a run goes the more it costs to keep the car running, and the less of the tank you can afford. The 1 km warning and the pump screen's heading (**FUEL STOP · PRICES UP 17% ON THE LAST STOP**, in amber) say how much the prices are up, and `--autotest` logs it (`fuel stop 4 Prairie Pumps: 0.0 of 45 L, regular $5.29/L (+23% on the stop before)`). `tools/price_test.tscn` (`godot --headless --path . tools/price_test.tscn`, 7 checks) tests the rounding and the limits over thousands of prices and rises.
  - **The LED signs (`scripts/led_board.gd`, `LedBoard`, and `shaders/led_board.gdshader`):** a price is shown the way a real fuel station's sign shows it, in **seven-segment LED figures** (three before the point and two after, so up to 999.99; the ones that are not needed show as the faint ghost of an 8, as on a real board) on a dark face with a thin frame. Each row is one quad drawn by one shader, so a price is two shader parameters and no geometry: the lit bars are bright amber with a hotter core and a soft glow round them (above 1 in HDR, so they bloom on High graphics and are still plain bright on the others), made of tiny LED dots when you are close, which fade out with distance; from far off, where the figures cannot be seen any more, the whole face of the board glows amber, so a sign can be seen from a long way and read from nearer. The figures glow harder at night (`GasStation.set_night`, driven by the time of day through `Scenery.set_night`) and the brand's colour on the pylon's header and the canopy's fascia is lit from inside as well. The **pylon** (16.7 m tall, 46 m before the pumps, facing the traffic) has the brand's colour and the station's name in a lit header, then a board headed PRICE PER LITRE with a row for each grade (REG, MID, PREM against the left margin and 1.4 m tall figures against the right). The **approach signs** (600 m and 300 m before the pumps) are 7.2 by 5.8 m, FUEL and the distance, the station's name, and the same three rows with 1 m figures. The old ones were amber letters on a flat board. `tools/led_test.tscn` (`xvfb-run godot --path . --rendering-method mobile --rendering-driver vulkan tools/led_test.tscn -- --out=/tmp/led`) shoots a wall of rows from near, middle distance, far and at night; with `-- --checks` (headless) it checks the figures a price puts in the cells. The Garage shows each car's fuel and tank size, and how far a tank goes (its range: 13 km in the Kestrel to 21 in the Vector).
  - Bosses and police pursuits never start near a fuel stop (and the speed checks are never at one). Choosing the upgrade after a boss clears the road: every car from 80 m behind to 260 m ahead vanishes and nothing new appears for a second, so you can get a hand back on the wheel. The upgrades on offer (three picked at random from this pool) are **Turbo kit**, **Big brakes**, **Sticky tyres**, **Long gears**, **Spare wheel** (+1 life), **Show-off** (+15% points), **Adrenaline**, **Pit crew**, **Crumple zone** (the next hit costs no life), **Roll cage** (1.5 s longer protection after every hit), **Rolling roadblock** (traffic 10% slower), **Eco tune** (25% less fuel), **Radar jammer** (a warning 250 m before a police speed check) and **Sponsor deal** ($400 per stage as an instant bonus), and seventeen more: **Nitrous kit** (+10% acceleration, +3% top speed), **Aero package** (+8% grip, +2% top speed), **Spotter** (near misses count from 1.3 m out, razor passes from 45 cm), **Showman** (the multiplier climbs 30% faster), **Cool head** (a hit keeps half your combo), **Speed demon** (the speed bonus from 100 km/h, paying 50% more), **Overdrive** (points count 25% more above 160 km/h), **Bounty hunter** (double boss prize money), **Psych-out** (rival bosses score 15% less), **Police scanner** (pursuits last 30 s instead of 40), **Friends in blue** (escaping the police also pays $500), **Fuel card** (30% off at the pump), **Mileage sponsor** (distance pays double), **Clean licence** ($150 for every kilometre without a hit), **Armoured panels** (half the damage from every knock), **Mechanic on call** (damage slows the car half as much) and **Quiet roads** (20% fewer cars). The ones that would only do the same again (all but Nitrous kit and Aero package, of the new ones) are offered once a run (`"once"` in `Data.BOONS`; `Run.taken` keeps what was chosen). `--boon=ID` takes any of them after every boss. Beating a boss can offer **Pit crew** (a free full tank) as one of the three upgrades when your tank isn't full.

### Build for Android

The project includes an export preset (`export_presets.cfg`) for a standard APK. It doesn't need Gradle or Android Studio's build system.

1. **Export templates:** in Godot, choose **Editor > Manage Export Templates > Download and Install**.
2. **Java and the Android SDK:** install a JDK (17 or newer) and the Android SDK. Android Studio is the easiest way to get the SDK; you need **build-tools** and **platform-tools** from its SDK Manager.
3. **Editor Settings > Export > Android:** set **Java SDK Path** and **Android SDK Path**. Godot creates the debug keystore itself the first time; if that field is empty, see the table below.
4. **Project > Export:** select **Android** and click **Export Project** to get `build/redline-rush.apk`. Or plug in a phone with USB debugging on and press the Android button in the editor's top-right corner.

Portrait orientation, immersive full screen and the vibration permission are already set.

**If the export fails**, the message at the bottom of the Export window usually names the cause:

| Message mentions | Fix |
| --- | --- |
| export templates / "No export template found" | Step 1, and make sure the templates match your Godot version exactly. |
| Android build template / Gradle | Turn **Use Gradle Build** off in the Android preset (it is off in the included preset). |
| Android SDK path / apksigner / build-tools | Step 3. The path must contain `build-tools/<version>/apksigner` and `platform-tools/adb`. |
| Java / JDK | Point **Java SDK Path** at the JDK folder (the one containing `bin/java`). |
| keystore | Leave the debug keystore fields empty so Godot creates one, or create one yourself: `keytool -genkeypair -keystore debug.keystore -storepass android -alias androiddebugkey -keypass android -keyalg RSA -validity 10000 -dname "CN=Android Debug"` |
| ETC2 / ASTC texture compression | Already enabled in `project.godot`. If it was turned off, re-enable **Rendering > Textures > VRAM Compression > Import ETC2 ASTC**. |

Command-line export, once the editor settings are in place:

```bash
godot --headless --path . --export-debug "Android" build/redline-rush.apk
```

**For playing, export a release build.** The debug export carries the debugger and its checks, which cost the scripts some speed (and so some heat). The release export needs a release keystore: the debug one signs it the same way, so it installs over the debug build as an update:

```bash
GODOT_ANDROID_KEYSTORE_RELEASE_PATH=path/to/debug.keystore \
GODOT_ANDROID_KEYSTORE_RELEASE_USER=androiddebugkey \
GODOT_ANDROID_KEYSTORE_RELEASE_PASSWORD=android \
godot --headless --path . --export-release "Android" build/redline-rush.apk
```

### Test flags

Arguments placed after `--` on the command line:

- `--autotest`: the car drives itself and the run loops, with status printed to the console. Combine it with `--headless` to run without a window.
- `--fast`: shrinks stage and boss targets so you reach a boss within seconds.
- `--cop`: the next encounter is always a police pursuit.
- `--no-traps`: no police speed checks on the road (the measurements that are not about them use it: `tools/boss_cal.py` and `tools/economy_sim.py` do). `--trap-at=METRES`: the first speed check is at the first place there is one at least that far into the run. `--speeding`: the test driver does not slow down for a speed check, so it starts a chase. `--radar`: every run starts with the radar jammer fitted. `--boon=ID`: with `--autotest`, the upgrade taken after every boss is this one (`radar`, `accel`... the ids in `Data.BOONS`), whether or not it is one of the three offered.
- `--cop-block=KIND` (implies `--cop`): the blocking exploit, as it was played. When the pursuit starts the player sits still in its lane behind a stopped car with the police car behind that, and the autotest logs `cop BUSTED after 5.8 s` when the police get round and ram it (or `ESCAPED` after 40 s if they cannot). KIND: `queue` (one stopped car behind the player), `queue2` (two), `truck`, `side` (a second stopped car beside the first, so one way round is shut), `wall` (a stopped car in every lane: no way round at all, the police car waits), `edge` (the player in the outermost lane), `far` (the player brakes to a stop from its speed with a car following it in, the police car coming up from where it started), `overshoot` (the player stops dead and the police car shoots past and has to back up).
- `--crash-at=SECONDS`: trigger the last-life crash at that time.
- `--collide-at=SECONDS:KIND[:LIVES]`: at that time clear the road around the player and set up a real collision that the game finds by itself: `KIND` is `rear`, `rear_off` (0.9 m to one side), `corner`, `side` (alongside, drifting in), `truck`, `van` or `rail` (nose into the guardrail); `LIVES` is what the player has left (default 1, so it is the last-life crash). With `--autotest` the blow and then the wreck are logged (`[autotest] impact ...`, `[autotest] crash t=... roll=... pitch=... cam=(...)`).
- `--crash-swipe=SECONDS:DX:DY[:FLICK[:PINCH]]`: that far into a crash, a finger lands in the middle of the screen and drags DX, DY (shares of the screen's width and height) through the real input path, a move a frame; `FLICK` 1 lets go while still moving, `PINCH` (a factor, 1.5 spreads them half as far again) puts down a second finger and spreads the two (`--collide-at=8:corner:1 --crash-swipe=1:0.25:-0.1`).
- `--music=N` or `--music=NAME`: every chase plays this song (its number from 1, or part of its name: `--music=slab`); without it the songs are picked at random. With `--autotest` each chase logs `chase music: Beneath The Slab (song 2 of 4, from 18.0 s)`.
- `--dent=KIND:STRENGTH[,...]`: the garage car takes knocks (`front`, `rear`, `left`, `right` or `corner`; strength is the change of speed in m/s, 3 a tap and 10 a hard knock), to see the crash damage without crashing (the garage car only, and nothing is saved).
- `--pit-test=SPEED:REL:X`: at the first fuel stop, put the car SPEED m/s fast, REL metres from the pumps (negative is before them) and X metres across the road (9.2 is the middle of the exit lane), hold that speed, and let it find its own way in; with `--autotest` the pull-in is logged (`[autotest] pit start ...`, then the stop at the pump). e.g. `--autotest --fast --first-station=300 --pit-test=55:-80:9.2`.
- `--shadow-atlas=N` / `--noglow`: the sun's shadow map size, and no bloom, whatever the graphics level (to see what they cost in looks).
- `--demo-pops`: shows sample scoring messages from small to big, to see how the text grows with the points.
- `--save-sfx=DIR`: writes every synthesised sound effect into DIR as a WAV file, then carries on.
- `--hitboxes`: draw every car's hit box as lines.
- `--perf[=FRAMES]`: times the sections of each frame (microseconds, averaged) and every FRAMES frames (default 600) prints them with what the renderer was asked to draw: triangles, objects and draw calls for the main view, its shadow pass and the rear-view mirror, and how many triangles the scene holds by kind (`PerfProbe`). Headless it measures the script side; under a window the draw numbers are the real renderer's.
- `--hitch=MS`: prints every frame of a run that took MS milliseconds or more, from the end of the one before (so the engine's work and the wait for the screen are in it, not only the script's), with where the script's time went, what the engine says it spent (process, physics, objects, nodes) and what the game noted in it (`PerfProbe.note`), and at the end a summary: the median, 99th percentile and worst frame, and how often each noted thing was in a hitch. The first 120 frames (a run's start, where things load) are not counted: `HITCH_SKIP=N` changes that and `HITCH_ALL=1` counts them all; what is done before the first frame is printed on its own line. Headless it measures what the script and the engine do, not what the graphics card does: `godot --headless --path . --fixed-fps 60 --quit-after 5000 -- --autotest --fast --godmode --seed=3 --hitch=8 --cop --start-stage=2`.
- `--seed=N`, `--shot-frame=FRAME:PATH`, `--state-log=EVERY`, `--start-hour=H`: with Godot's own `--fixed-fps` a run repeats exactly, so two builds can be compared frame for frame. `--seed` fixes the random world, `--shot-frame` saves a screenshot at that frame (rather than at a time), `--state-log` prints the state of the simulation every EVERY frames (the player, the sums over the traffic, the run) and `--start-hour` starts the clock at that hour and lets it run (unlike `--hour`, which holds it). For example, to check that a change did not touch the simulation, and what it did to the script time:
- `--replay-at=SECONDS[:CAM]`: open the replay editor at that time, optionally with a camera (`chase`, `hood`, `cockpit`, `free`, `keys`, `tv`, `drone`).
- `--stations=KM` / `--first-station=M` / `--fuel=FRACTION`: a fuel stop every KM km (the tank range shrinks to match), the first stop M metres in, and starting with that fraction of a tank.
- `--start-stage=N` / `--godmode`: start runs at stage N, and never lose lives (not saved). With `--autotest` this prints each boss race's scores.
- `--quit-after=SECONDS`: exit after that many seconds.
- `--open=garage` / `--open=garage:TAB`: open the Garage on start (`cars`, `up` for the Workshop's Upgrades, `paint`, `wheels` or `repair`); `--open=repair` then pays for the selected car's repair. `--open=ACTION[:ARG]` runs any menu action (`browse:1` for the next car in the Garage, `paint:#c4161c`, `finish:candy`, `rim:blade`, `select:vector`, `map`, `settings:camera`...), one after another if repeated, all as the game starts. `--open-at=SECONDS:ACTION[:ARG]` does the same once that many seconds have passed (`--open=garage:cars --open-at=2:browse:-1` shows the car sliding in from the left), and `--open=scroll:PIXELS` scrolls the Workshop's list (`--open=garage:paint --open=scroll:520` shows the colours) and `--open-at=2:swipe:-1` swipes a finger to the left across the Cars page.
- `--wear=F`: the car you drive starts with that wear (0 to 1; 1 is a wreck, which a test run repairs by itself, so this is for seeing the wreck screens), `--hold-over` keeps the run-over screen up (an autotest run does not restart), `--inspect-finish=ID` puts a paint finish on the car in the inspect showroom (`gloss`, `matte`, `metallic`, `pearl`, `candy`, `chrome`, ...). Environment variables for the computer drivers (with `--autotest`): `BOSS_DEBUG=1` prints the boss's hardest braking and any jump in its speed, `BRAKE_DEBUG=1` each time a traffic car brakes harder than 7 m/s² or is snapped to the speed of the car in front (where, who is ahead of it, how far and how fast), `BOSS_PLAN=T0:T1` prints what the boss's planner sees and decides between those seconds of a race, `COP_DEBUG=1` prints the police car's state every quarter second, any frames it is inside a car and each time the guard steps in, and `COP_PLAN=T0:T1` its planner's rounds.
- `--target-scale=X`: the stage and boss targets times X (`--fast` is 0.08; a big one, 1000, means no boss turns up). `--autolook=SECONDS`: the test driver brakes when the road ahead is shorter than that many seconds of travel (0, the default, is the old 16 m, which is too little above 50 m/s; `--autospeed=M/S` sets how fast it tries to go). `--odometer=METRES`: with `--autotest`, print the run's time, mean speed, score, hits and fuel every that many metres. `--flat-traffic`: traffic that does not thin out above 50 m/s. With `--autotest` every boss race also logs an economy line (`[autotest] economy: car pulse route 0 stage 1 WON | 131 s, 5212 m, mean 143 km/h, hits 3 | score 7017 ... | gross $479 = score $175 + distance $104 + prizes $200 | fuel 14.0 L burnt, $0 paid`).
- `--boss-sk=X` / `--boss-edge=X`: every boss races at skill X, and its driving is scored with generosity X, whatever the highway and stage (`--boss-edge=1.0` is how `tools/boss_cal.py` measures what a skill earns). With `--route=R --start-stage=S --autotest` each boss race prints its skill, its score generosity (`edge`), the two scores and what the rival did.
- `--shot=SECONDS:PATH`: save a screenshot.
- `--cam=chase|hood|cockpit`: start in that camera view (not saved). `--cam=inspect` looks across the cabin from the back seat.
- `--route=N`: drive highway N (0 to 13), opening it if needed.
- `--hour=H`: hold the clock at that hour (0 to 24) to check the light at any time of day.
- `--open=settings` (or `--open=settings:controls|display|dev` for a tab, `--open=settings:camera` for the camera adjuster) / `--open=pedal_edit`: open the settings screen, the camera adjuster or the pedal editor on start. `--open=refuel_demo` shows the pump screen at a stop where the fuel has climbed to $56.59 a litre (to see that the big prices fit), `--open=pick_demo` shows the upgrade pick screen with its three wordiest upgrades, and `--hold-pick` (with `--autotest`) stays on it after a boss instead of taking the first upgrade, to look at it.
- `--car=ID` / `--autospeed=M/S`: test-drive another car (not saved) and set how fast the test driver goes.
- `--steer=swipe|wheel|buttons|tilt|stick` (with `--fake-tilt=DEGREES` to simulate tilting) / `--orient=portrait|landscape`: try a steering method or orientation without saving it.
- `--steer-test=flick|full|lane|pass` (with `--steer-speed=M/S` and `--steer-assist`): a scripted hand drives the real steering on an empty road and logs what the car does (`[steer] ...` lines); `pass` lets the chosen control's own value through, e.g. `--steer=tilt --fake-tilt=2 --steer-test=pass --open=set:dz_tilt=3` checks a dead zone. `godot --headless --path . --script tools/steer_test.gd` measures the steering model and the tilt filter on their own: how quickly the car moves over after a flick, how long a lane change takes, how late and how steady the tilt command is.
- `--idcolor`: draw each traffic car in a flat unique colour with no fog or scenery; with `--burst` it also logs where each car should be, to measure flicker.
- `--demo-pops`: show a sample score message every second, to check where messages appear in each camera view.
- `--burst=SECONDS:COUNT:DIR`: save COUNT consecutive frames from SECONDS onwards (for checking flicker).
- `--showroom` / `--showroom=front` / `--showroom=close` (or `t_rear` / `t_front` for traffic models; `--inspect=t_SHAPE:…` frames a single traffic model): park every player and boss car in a row with the game frozen, to inspect the models.
- `--showroom=cam:X,Y,Z,TX,TY,TZ[,FOV]`, `--sc-base=M`, `--sc-finish=M`: a free camera in the frozen showroom (road space: x across, z = minus the distance ahead), looking at the road as it is M metres into a run (`--sc-base`, so a sign, an overpass or a fuel stop can be looked at close up; with `--autotest --first-station=M` the fuel stop is there too) and with the finish gantry at M metres (`--sc-finish`). e.g. `--showroom=cam:0,5.4,-402,0,6.4,-420,36` looks at the first exit gantry.
- `--inspect=CAR:YAWS:PITCH:DIST[:FOV]`: one car on its own, with the camera orbiting it at the given angles in degrees (several yaws separated by commas step every 3 seconds). Combine with `--win=WxH` (and Godot's `--resolution WxH`) for large screenshots.
